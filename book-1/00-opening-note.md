# A Note Before the Design

This note is me speaking. So are the fifth part of this book and the short part
at its end. Everything between them was made differently, and the difference is
the reason this book exists.

Here is what you are holding. It is a design for a society — not a case for one,
not a story about one, and not a plan for getting to one from here. It describes
a place where certain things are owed to every person from the day they exist,
where nothing has to happen first — no work, no papers, no belonging, no good
behaviour — and where the machinery that could take those things away has been
worked on until most of the taking became impossible to even write down.

I say "worked on" because that is the honest verb. The society in these pages
exists as an actual set of rules, and the rules were checked — by a machine that
does not care what I hoped they would do, only what they do. When a rule
contradicted the design's own promises, the machine refused it, and some of
those refusals changed my mind about what I was building. The order of the
chapters is not mine either: each chapter rests only on what came before it,
because that is the order the rules themselves stand in. If you want to see this
machinery, the last part of the book shows all of it. If you never want to see
it, you will never need to — every chapter in between is written to be read, not
decoded.

Now the warning, and it is the most important thing on this page.

This book confesses. Constantly. You will find a chapter that calls one of the
society's own rules indefensible. You will find an alarm that never once worked,
a debt that nothing collects, machinery that notices wrongs and then stops. You
will find the sentence "the design does not know" more often than you have ever
seen it in a book that is arguing *for* something.

Read every one of those confessions as the product working. The subtitle of this
book is not "a society without failures"; it is a design worked out *to the
point where it catches its own failures* — and catching them means printing
them. A promise that cannot show you where it breaks is not a promise, it is
advertising, and you have read enough advertising. The first readers of this
book missed this, and I do not blame them — one finished the whole thing
convinced it was a warning, a beautiful nightmare built to show what not to do.
It is the opposite. Every flaw in these pages is declared because a declared
flaw can be worked on, and the working-on is the point. Where a flaw was fixed,
the chapter tells you it was fixed and what it cost. Where it stands open, the
chapter says that too, in the plainest words I could find.

And one more honesty, the deepest one. Checking a design can make its
commitments precise. It cannot make them right. The machine can tell you exactly
what follows from what this society writes down about a person, and it refuses
whole classes of cruelty outright — but nothing in logic says what people should
be owed in the first place. Nothing in logic says the floor beneath every person
should hold the right to speak and not, say, the right to water. Those choices
are mine, they are argued late in the book where argument belongs, and where I
could not defend a choice I have said so instead of defending it badly. Precision
is what I can promise you. Justification you will have to judge.

Read it in order — the chapters genuinely depend on each other. Stop at the end
of the fifth part if you are done; the book is complete there. Go on to the last
part if you want to see the machine, the refusals, and what it felt like to be
told no by my own design.

The poem on the previous page asks whether a life must be spent searching for
the day's meal. This book is the longest answer I could give.
