# Changing the Rules

A society that cannot change its rules is not stable, it is brittle. So this one can.
An assembly proposes a change, the electorate approves it, and the change becomes law.
Two steps, both on the record, neither requiring anybody's permission beyond the
approval itself.

Three things cannot be changed this way.

The floor — the eight things owed to every person. The rule that a prisoner is still a
person. And the list of what cannot be changed.

## Approved and dead

Watch what happens to an amendment that would cut the floor.

It is properly proposed. It is put to the electorate. The electorate approves it — not
narrowly, not procedurally; the approval is recorded and real. And it does not become
law.

Not vetoed. Not struck down afterwards by a court that took a different view. The
amendment has no standing from the moment it names what it intends to touch, and the
approval is simply irrelevant to its fate. You may count the votes for as long as you
like.

Compare an ordinary reform — a change to how contribution is recognised, say.
Proposed, approved, and it becomes law without incident. The machinery is not
obstructive. It is obstructive about exactly three things.

This is the design's answer to a specific historical failure: the constitution that
can lawfully dismantle itself. A framework whose every provision is amendable by
sufficient majority contains no protection at all against a sufficient majority, and
sufficient majorities have been assembled for terrible things by entirely lawful
means. The response here is not to trust that it will not happen. It is to place three
provisions outside the reach of the procedure.

## Why the list guards itself

The third entrenched item is the strange one and it is doing the most work.

Suppose the floor and the personhood rule were entrenched, and the register naming
them was not. Then the route is obvious: propose an amendment removing the floor from
the protected list. That amendment does not touch the floor — it touches the list. It
passes. Now propose the amendment cutting the floor. It also passes, because the floor
is no longer protected.

Two ordinary steps, each individually lawful, and the protection is gone. Every
entrenchment scheme that does not close this has a two-move defeat.

So the register protects itself. An amendment adjusting the list of protected things
is void on the same terms as an amendment adjusting the things themselves — which is
tested here, and it dies exactly like the floor cut did. The guard cannot be removed
through the front door.

## What this actually costs

Be plain about it: this is anti-democratic, deliberately.

Three provisions are placed beyond the reach of any majority, however large, however
sincere, however many times it assembles. People not yet born are bound by a decision
they had no part in. That is the objection, it is a serious one, and the design does
not have a clever answer to it.

What it has is a trade. Everything else — recognition, placement, procedure, the whole
apparatus described in this book — remains amendable, and the three protected items
are the ones without which the rest has no floor to stand on. The bet is that a
society is better served by a small permanent core it cannot argue with than by a
completeness that leaves nothing to fall back on when argument goes wrong.

You may think that bet is wrong. It is a bet, and it is the kind a constitution has to
make one way or the other; refusing to choose is choosing that everything is amendable.

## Three ways this is thinner than it looks

The protection is real and it rests on three things that are not.

**The check is self-declared.** An amendment is caught because it says what it
intends to adjust. Propose one that declares no target at all, and there is nothing
for the guard to compare against — it is proposed, approved, and enacted with no
examination of what it does. The entire entrenchment mechanism depends on amendments
honestly announcing their own subject, which is a strange thing for a mechanism
designed against bad faith to depend on.

**Nothing happens when something becomes law.** The machinery determines which
amendments are valid. That determination then sits there. No other rule in the entire
design consults it, changes behaviour because of it, or does anything with it
whatsoever. The society can tell you an amendment became law and it cannot tell you
what became different.

That is the same shape as the gap in Chapter 8 — an exact account of what is owed and
silence about arrival — and it is arguably worse here, because it is the *procedure*
for change rather than the substance. A constitution that can identify valid
amendments and cannot enact them has described a legislature rather than built one.

**And the protected list is a set of records that people keep.** Nothing derives
which items are entrenched; somebody wrote them down. The rules cannot prevent
somebody from un-writing them. There is no amendment required, no approval, no
proposal — just a line removed from a file, after which the floor cut passes normally
and nothing anywhere notes that the rules of amendment changed.

That is the third time this book has arrived at the same place from a different
direction, and it is worth stating without softening. **The strongest protection in
this design is the impossibility of writing certain rules. The weakest is the
integrity of the record those rules are written in.** Everything in the preceding
chapters sits on the second, and the second is people.
